## One-sentence definition

Biohacking is deliberately intervening in your own biology — and checking the result — in order to restore, enhance, or extend healthspan. This wiki groups the methods into ten domains.

That is this wiki’s working definition, used to file articles. It is not a legal, regulatory, or textbook official boundary.

## In plain language

Think of the body as a system you can measure and deliberately adjust. The aims usually fall into three kinds: bring a lost or damaged function back toward a usual level (restore); take a usual function past a typical level, or add a new one, when there is no such deficit (enhance); and postpone age-related decline so more years are lived in good health (extend). The methods come from ten domains: from recording data and adjusting light, cold, and heat, to molecules, genes, and cells, to neural interfaces and prostheses. The same method can serve more than one goal, and the same goal can be pursued with more than one method. This is not an officially defined discipline. Evidence, risk, and legal limits vary widely. This wiki records what can be checked; it does not recommend that you try something.

“Hacking,” here, does not mean breaking into a computer. It means you change a biological process on purpose, and you use feedback to see whether the change did anything. Feedback can be a measurement, or a change in function you can notice. An accidental act, with no aim and no look at the result, is not what this page means by biohacking.

Everyday health habits and ordinary medicine often use the same methods. The difference is usually not “which drug or device was used,” but who is directing the change, what it is for, whether anyone is watching the result, and whether the act sits inside routine care, or at the edge of or outside it. The next section draws that line.

## Three goals

A goal answers *why* someone uses a method. It is not itself a method. The same device can restore a lost function for one person and enhance a usual function for another. The three goals also overlap: repairing a damaged function often also helps someone stay healthy for longer. The full grid is on the [goals and methods table](/goals/).

### Restore

Restore means bringing a lost or damaged function back toward a usual level.

Examples: a prosthesis that helps someone who has lost a limb grasp again belongs under [bionics](/bionic/); some approved drugs or peptides used for a diagnosed condition belong under [biochemical](/biochemical/) or [peptides](/peptides/) — those uses are first of all medical care, and this wiki still files the method by how it works; some neural stimulation used for approved conditions belongs under [neurotech](/neurohacking/). [Quantified self](/quantified-self/) does not itself restore a function, but it can show where a damaged function stands now.

### Enhance

Enhance means taking a usual function past a typical level, or adding a new ability, when there is no matching deficit.

Examples: a person with no functional loss who has an identity chip placed under the skin belongs under [cyborg implants](/cyborg/); attempts to push attention or physical performance “past typical” with neural stimulation or certain small molecules land under [neurotech](/neurohacking/) or [biochemical](/biochemical/). Being filed under this goal is not a claim that the method is proven effective or safe. Many enhancement claims rest on weak evidence, and some are only personal reports.

### Extend

Extend means postponing age-related decline and lengthening healthspan — the years lived in good health. It is a goal, not an eleventh domain. Articles on the biology of aging sit under this goal; see the [longevity hub](/goals/longevity/).

Examples: keeping a long record of age-related change belongs under [quantified self](/quantified-self/); designed, measurable light or meal-timing protocols belong under [environmental signals](/lifestyle/); research paths that target mechanisms of aging may appear under [biochemical](/biochemical/), [peptides](/peptides/), [cellular and regenerative](/cellular/), and related domains. This wiki does not describe any supplement or procedure as proven to extend human lifespan.

## The ten domains are the methods

A method answers *what is used*. The ten domains are this wiki’s editorial map: a set of boxes for filing and cross-checking articles, not ten drawers that exist in nature. Peptides are a first-level domain, alongside biochemical methods. Brain-computer interfaces sit inside neurotech. Prostheses sit inside bionics.

A standalone version of the diagram is also available: [domain diagram](/diagrams/domain-map.svg). In the figure below, each card opens that domain’s landing page.

<DomainMap />

- [Quantified self](/quantified-self/): sensors and logs that turn body states into data you can compare. This domain is usually read-only — it does not itself change the body — but it supplies feedback for other methods.
- [Environmental signals](/lifestyle/): measurable physical or timing cues such as light, cold, and heat, used to shift the body’s own rhythms and stress responses, without adding an external substance.
- [Biochemical](/biochemical/): chemical molecules that are not amino-acid chains, such as nutrients, supplements, or small-molecule drugs.
- [Peptides](/peptides/): short amino-acid chains used as signals. This is a first-level domain, not a folder under biochemical methods. Purity and supply are separate questions to check.
- [Genetic](/genetic/): reading or rewriting DNA or RNA.
- [Cellular and regenerative](/cellular/): living cells or tissue used as replacement or repair material.
- [Microbiome](/microbiome/): changing the microbes that live with you, mainly in the gut.
- [Neurotech](/neurohacking/): reading or writing neural signals with electricity, magnetism, light, or electrodes.
- [Cyborg implants](/cyborg/): hardware placed in the body that does not read or write the brain, such as chips and magnets.
- [Bionics](/bionic/): machines that replace or attach to a limb or a sense.

## What counts, and what does not

In this wiki’s usage, the following are closer to “counting as biohacking”:

- You pursue at least one of restore, enhance, or extend, you choose methods from the domains above on purpose, and you look at feedback.
- This can happen at home, in a community lab, or in a clinic. Directing your own course does not mean avoiding clinicians. Having a clinician involved does not automatically take the practice out of this word.
- The same act can also be medicine, training, or research. This wiki files by method and goal. It does not issue credentials.

The following usually do not count, or are only background, rather than biohacking as this page uses the word:

- Everyday habits that are not designed as a measurable intervention — ordinary balanced meals, walking, going to bed on time. The environmental-signals domain covers dosed, trackable physical or timing protocols, not every form of “living well.”
- Standard treatment a clinician sets for a diagnosed disease. That is first of all medical care. Using a peptide or a device does not automatically rename it biohacking. This wiki may still file the method under its domain, so evidence and regulatory notes can be compared.
- Using “biohacking” only as a marketing word to sell a product that does not state evidence, risk, or legal limits.
- Computer intrusion that has nothing to do with intervening in a body. That is a different use of “hack.”

A practical distinction: ordinary fitness or wellness often stops at “completing the plan.” Here the emphasis is on a designed change to a biological system, and on checking what that change did. The two can overlap. The line is not a hard border.

## A spectrum: from everyday to the frontier

Biohacking is not only underground experiment, and it is not only expensive clinic work. It can be logging sleep and blood glucose, a planned change in light or cold exposure, an approved drug or prosthesis in a clinic, or a gene or brain-computer method that is still experimental.

Closer to everyday practice is not automatically lower risk. Closer to the frontier is not automatically better evidenced, and it is not automatically more helpful for a healthy person. What is legal depends on the place, the product’s authorization, and the specific use. A substance with the same name is not the same product when it is an approved drug and when it is an unapproved item labeled “for research.” See [evidence and safety](/overview/evidence-and-safety/).

## Evidence and risk

This wiki’s stance is to record, not to promote. Articles grade evidence by study design. A “frontier” label is not a claim that something is proven. Pages do not give dosing, how-to steps, or buying directions. For a personal decision, read the labeling of an approved product and consult a qualified clinician; see the [medical disclaimer](/disclaimer/).

Evidence varies by method. Some approaches have human randomized trials; some have only animal or laboratory data; some have only personal reports. Risk also includes quality, dose, interactions, infection or implant complications, and legal or regulatory consequences.

For example, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration states on a public page that unapproved GLP-1 drugs have not been reviewed for safety, effectiveness, and quality before marketing, and on another page it flags certain bulk substances nominated for compounding as possibly posing significant safety risks. This wiki cites those pages to show what a regulator has already said, not to recommend or dismiss a particular personal use. A fuller summary is on [evidence and safety](/overview/evidence-and-safety/).

## Common misconceptions

- **It is a formal academic field.** It is not. Published research also treats it as a diverse set of practices, not one official profession. A paper in *Medical Humanities* describes biohacking as a do-it-yourself approach to optimising body and mind, and discusses four strands: DIYbio, spiritual augmentation, health and wellness optimisation, and technological futurism. That is a research classification, not an official definition, and not this wiki’s ten domains. This wiki files by goal and method so articles can be cross-checked. It does not replace other maps.
- **Only illegal or underground practice counts.** It does not. One end of the spectrum is everyday, measurable adjustment; the other includes clinic-grade and still-experimental technology. Legality has to be checked separately.
- **Using a frontier method means it already works.** It does not. Novelty is not the same as sufficient evidence.
- **Longevity is an eleventh domain.** It is not. It is one of the three goals.
- **Peptides are only a subset of biochemical methods.** Not on this wiki. Peptides are a first-level domain.
- **Starting to measure already improves the body.** It does not. Quantified self makes change visible; whether anything improved is a separate question.
- **This wiki is teaching you how to do it.** It is not. It is a reference for looking things up and comparing sources, not a how-to manual or a product catalog.
